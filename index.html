What this is

A worksheet that estimates how much money a seller actually walks away with, at three different sale prices, for a house in unincorporated King County or the city of Renton.

Most online calculators give you one number and hide the assumptions. This one shows every deduction as its own line so you can argue with any of them, and it separates the money you receive at the closing table from the money you keep after taxes. Those are two different numbers that arrive at two different times, and treating them as one is the most common way sellers get surprised.

How to use it

  1. Enter three prices you're actually considering. They should be close together. The point is to see what a $20,000 or $45,000 price change really costs you, which is never the full amount.
  2. Fill in the costs of sale. Commission percentages come from your listing agreement. Fixed closing costs come from an escrow quote or a net sheet.
  3. Set the closing date. It drives the property tax proration and the capital gains holding period. Both change with the calendar.
  4. Fill in the capital gain section. This is the part most calculators skip, and for a recently purchased home it can be the largest number on the page.
  5. Read the two bottom lines. The green figure is cash at closing. The dark figure is what you keep after federal tax.

Everything recalculates as you type. Nothing is saved and nothing is sent anywhere; the file runs entirely in your browser.

Every field, explained

Sale prices

FieldWhat to enter
Scenario 1, 2, 3Three prices you're weighing. Scenario 1 becomes the baseline that the other two are compared against. Use the price you expect to actually sell at, not the list price, if you expect to negotiate.

Costs of sale

FieldWhat to enter
Listing agentThe percentage your own agent's brokerage charges. From your listing agreement. Negotiable.
Buyer agentWhat you're offering to the buyer's agent. Since 2024 this is no longer advertised through the MLS and is negotiated deal by deal, but most sellers still pay something. Also negotiable, and often the first thing to move in a slow market.
Fixed closing costsEscrow fee, title insurance policy, recording fee, document preparation. Ask escrow for a quote or take it from an agent's net sheet. It barely changes with sale price, which is why it's a flat dollar figure here.
Buyer concessionsMoney you agree to credit the buyer, usually toward their loan costs or to settle a repair request after inspection. Enter zero at listing, then update it once you're in negotiation. This line is where deals quietly get more expensive.
Mortgage payoffWhat you owe the lender. Not your statement balance. Request a written payoff quote good through your closing date; it includes interest accruing to that day plus a reconveyance or release fee, so it always comes in higher than the balance you see online.

Closing date and property tax proration

FieldWhat to enter
Closing dateThe day the sale records. Drives both the tax proration and the capital gains holding period.
Annual property taxYour full-year bill. Look up the parcel on the King County Assessor's eReal Property tool, or read it off your most recent tax statement.
Paid so far this yearWhether you've paid nothing, the first half, or the full year. If a lender pays your taxes out of escrow, check with them rather than assuming.

You owe property tax for the days you owned the house. If you've prepaid past your closing date, the buyer reimburses you and the line shows as a credit in green. If you're behind, you're debited at settlement. It is almost never zero, which is why a net sheet showing $0.00 on this line is a placeholder rather than a calculation.

Federal capital gain

FieldWhat to enter
Purchase dateWhen you bought. This determines whether you qualify for the exclusion described below, so get it exactly right.
Purchase priceWhat you paid, from your closing statement.
Buy-side closing costsCosts you paid when buying that add to your basis: title, escrow, recording, transfer taxes, survey, legal. Not loan points, prepaid interest, or prepaid property tax.
Capital improvementsMoney spent on things that add value or extend the life of the house: a new roof, a remodeled kitchen, an added bathroom, new landscaping hardscape. Not repairs, not maintenance, not painting. Keep receipts; the IRS asks for them, not for your memory.
Filing statusSets the exclusion cap: $250,000 single or married filing separately, $500,000 married filing jointly.
Long-term rateYour federal long-term capital gains bracket: 0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on total taxable income. Most sellers are at 15%.
Ordinary rate if short-termOnly used when you've owned the house a year or less. In that case the gain is taxed as regular income at your marginal bracket, not at capital gains rates.
3.8% net investment income taxAn extra federal surtax that applies to investment income above certain income thresholds. Check with your CPA if your household income is high.
Partial exclusionIf you're selling before the 2-year mark because of a job relocation, a health issue, or another qualifying unforeseen circumstance, you may claim a prorated share of the exclusion. Checking this box prorates the cap by days held.
Already claimed within 2 yearsThe exclusion can only be used once every two years. If you used it on another home sale recently, check this and the exclusion drops to zero.

How the excise tax works

Washington charges the seller a real estate excise tax, or REET, on the full sale price. Not the profit, not the equity, not the amount above the mortgage. Sellers who break even still owe it in full.

It's tiered, so different slices of the price are taxed at different rates. Only the portion inside each band is taxed at that band's rate. The rates below combine the state's graduated schedule with the 0.50% local rate that applies in both unincorporated King County and the city of Renton.

Portion of sale priceCombined rate
Up to $525,0001.60% (1.10% state + 0.50% local)
$525,000.01 to $1,525,0001.78% (1.28% state + 0.50% local)
$1,525,000.01 to $3,025,0003.25% (2.75% state + 0.50% local)
Above $3,025,0003.50% (3.00% state + 0.50% local)

Worked example at a $925,000 sale price:

First $525,000 × 1.60% = $8,400.00
Next $400,000 × 1.78% = $7,120.00
 
Total excise tax = $15,520.00
Effective rate = 1.678%

Because the tiers are marginal rather than all-or-nothing, pricing just under a threshold saves less than most people expect. The saving is only the rate difference on the amount above the line, not a rate change on the whole sale.

Verify before closing. The Department of Revenue readjusts these price thresholds periodically, and the current values took effect January 1, 2023. Local rates are republished on their own schedule. If your closing moves into a new year, pull the current rate sheet from the Department of Revenue rather than trusting this page.

The two-year rule, in plain English

Section 121 of the tax code lets you exclude up to $250,000 of gain from federal tax if you're single, or $500,000 if you're married filing jointly. It is the largest tax break most homeowners will ever get.

To qualify, you must have owned the home and lived in it as your main home for at least 2 years out of the 5 years before the sale. The two years don't have to be consecutive.

Three consequences worth understanding before you set a closing date:

  • It's a cliff, not a slope. At 729 days you get nothing and the entire gain is taxable. At 730 days you get the full exclusion. Nothing else in this worksheet behaves that way, and no commission negotiation is worth as much as clearing that date.
  • Under one year is worse still. Owning for 12 months or less makes the gain short-term, which means it's taxed as ordinary income at your regular bracket instead of at capital gains rates.
  • There are exceptions. Selling early because of a job relocation, a health problem, or certain unforeseen circumstances can earn you a prorated portion of the exclusion. The rules are specific. Ask a CPA rather than assuming you qualify.

Each results card carries a colored flag showing how long the home has been held and the exact date the two-year test is met.

Washington itself does not tax this. The state's capital gains excise tax specifically excludes all real estate, primary residence and investment property alike. Only the federal tax applies.

What this worksheet does not include

  • HOA dues, transfer fees, and estoppel or resale certificate charges
  • Per-diem interest and reconveyance fees inside the loan payoff
  • Repair credits negotiated after the inspection, beyond whatever you enter as concessions
  • Home warranty, pest inspection, and other seller-elective items
  • Junior liens, judgments, second mortgages, or HELOC balances
  • Moving costs, storage, staging, and pre-listing repairs
  • Depreciation recapture, which applies if the home was ever a rental
  • State and local income tax, since Washington has none on this
